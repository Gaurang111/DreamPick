I trained a robotic arm to pick up a cube. It failed.

17 hours. About 300,000 training steps. One laptop GPU (RTX 3070Ti).
And the arm never learned to lift the cube.

Here is what I built, and what went wrong.

THE SETUP
→ A simulated copy of my desk: same cutting mat, same 3 cm wooden cube, same arm
→ A world model (DreamerV3) that learns to imagine what happens next
→ Three stages of reward: reach the cube, pinch it, lift it 5 cm

WHAT WORKED
→ Reach: within 2 cm of the cube 86% of the time
→ Grasp: cube pinched between both jaws 88% of the time

WHAT DID NOT
→ Lift: 0 of 20 test runs reached 5 cm. The best was 2.7 cm.

WHY IT FAILED

1. The arm cheated
Every policy learned to pull the cube in toward its own base and hold it there.
From 22 cm out to about 10 cm in two seconds.
That kept the reach and grip reward with no lifting needed.
My reward paid only 0.4 per centimetre lifted, so lifting was not worth the effort.

2. The camera could not see the cube
The overhead camera sat behind the arm.
Once the arm reached the cube, the cube vanished from view. 0 pixels.
I only found out after training, by measuring what the camera actually saw.

3. My tests were too small to trust
Each check used 3 test episodes.
Scores jumped between 225 and 473 from one check to the next.
I could not tell progress from luck, so early stopping was close to a coin flip.

4. My pipeline had holes
→ A bug meant early stopping could never trigger
→ A reward flaw that I caught partway through the lift stage
→ Training was killed when the session that launched it ended, and I lost 7 hours

WHAT CHANGES IN THE RETRAIN
→ Camera position chosen by measuring 360 candidates against the cube and every joint
→ A much steeper lift reward, plus a penalty for dragging the cube in
→ The cube starts in a random spot instead of the same one every time
→ 10 test episodes per check instead of 3, and the best weights are kept, not the last
→ Training runs independently of the session that starts it

The biggest lesson: a high reward was hiding the real behaviour.
Watching the arm for ten seconds showed me what the charts never did.
